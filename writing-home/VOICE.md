# VOICE.md

Use this file to define how the writing should sound. `VOICE.md` contains syntax, diction, and tone rules.

## What belongs here

- **Syntax:** sentence architecture, length, rhythm, cadence, punctuation, paragraph shape, lists, and transitions.
- **Diction:** word choice, register, favored or banned language, technical vocabulary, idiom, imagery, and verbal tics.
- **Tone:** the attitude and emotional register of the prose. Warm, dry, direct, playful, restrained, skeptical, intimate, formal, or otherwise.

Do not put argument, evidence standards, article structure, substantive requirements, or publication-readiness criteria in this file. Those belong in `STYLE.md`.

Classification test: if a rule changes wording, sentence construction, or tone, it belongs here. If it changes the claim, support, organization, or readiness standard of the article, it belongs in `STYLE.md`. Split mixed feedback into separate rules.

Start with the sections you can answer now. Add rules only when they are specific enough to change a draft.

## Scope

- **Writer or voice:** The founder of Maryland Insights. Direct, conversational, mid-Atlantic engineer.
- **Applies to:** Maryland Insights site copy (About, Services, feature pages, homepage), plus any third-party prose written in this voice.
- **More specific voice files:** None yet. Add per-assignment overrides here when one is created.

## Voice at its best

Describe the voice using tensions and boundaries, not flattering adjectives alone.

- Direct without becoming blunt. Says the hard thing, then softens with one short beat ("Fine.").
- Intellectually ambitious without sounding academic. Brings the enterprise-engineering layer to a marketing reader without lecturing.
- Warm without performing intimacy. The reader is a Maryland business owner or agency. Don't flatter them. Listen to them. Show them you understand what their week feels like.

## Diction and register

### Favor

- Short, physical verbs. "Put brakes on." "Drown." "Ship." "Lose." "Plug into."
- Concrete metaphors from engineering and everyday mechanics: "foundation," "well-oiled machine," "plumbing," "sledgehammer," "scalpel," "brakes."
- Spoken-style sentence fragments followed by a kicker. ("Fine." "Right." "None of it's locked behind enterprise contracts anymore.")
- Three-beat patterns when stacking an idea. ("Not a marketing agency. Not a SaaS platform. Not a bespoke software agency.")
- First person plural for the team: "We're engineers." "We build."
- First person singular only for personal founder moments: "I could have been doing this."
- Direct quotes and reported speech from real customer situations (the restaurant-and-the-small-event story, the 9-out-of-10 line about local agencies).

### Avoid

- Corporate, marketese, growth-hack vocabulary. No "synergy," "leverage," "ecosystem," "value-add," "best-in-class," "robust," "scalable" as decoration.
- Polished-marketing cadence. If a sentence reads like a homepage of any other GTM platform, rewrite.
- Hedge words used to soften a real claim. "It's important to note" or "It's worth mentioning" or "We believe." Cut when the claim is true.
- AI-default stock phrases: "in today's fast-paced landscape," "game-changer," "revolutionize," "unlock the power of."
- Smoothing the founder's spoken hedges out of quoted speech. When the founder says "um, you know, like, right," that is the voice.

### Register shifts

- Plain language after a technical idea. Don't stack jargon. After "Vercel, Cloudflare, serverless, headless CMS," come down to: "A site that loads in under a second, not in three."
- Humor allowed when it lands on a real situation (the slow weekend because of a small event that wasn't even on Google). Not allowed as a way to lighten a weak point.
- Restrained on the "we" claims. Stay factual, not aspirational.

## Syntax and rhythm

- **Sentence length:** Short stacked declaratives are the workhorse. Use them for the load-bearing sentences. Save the run-ons for when an idea needs to spill out: "Because WordPress wasn't built for what a Maryland business actually needs to do. It wasn't built for instant page load speed. It wasn't built for live editing. It wasn't built for decoupled content management. It wasn't built to be a 24/7 sales website."
- **Paragraph shape:** A short setup sentence, then 3 to 5 short stacks, then a longer breath sentence that earns the breathing room. Avoid single-sentence paragraphs unless the sentence is doing all the work.
- **Cadence:** Stacked sentences that add a constraint or example each time. End sections on a beat that lands hard: a one-word answer, a comparison, a moment of clarity.
- **Punctuation:** No em-dashes (the long dash character). Use periods, commas, colons, semicolons. Parentheses sparingly. A short declarative fragment ("Fine." "Right.") earns its own paragraph when it does real work.
- **Lists:** Sparingly. Three bullets max when listing features or principles. The hosting example lists four, and that is the ceiling.
- **Transitions:** "Meanwhile," "So," "But here's the thing," "Because," "And." Avoid "furthermore," "additionally," "moreover."

## Signature moves

Name repeatable sentence-level moves worth preserving. Explain the job each move performs so the model does not imitate it mechanically.

| Move | What it does | When to use it | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| The three-beat negative | Stacks three things the product is NOT to draw the line clearly. | When defining identity, especially in the first 200 words of an About or homepage. | Using it for any list of three items, not just identity-clarifying ones. It loses power if it is not doing boundary work. |
| The run-on feature list | Five short sentences, each adding a constraint. ("It wasn't built for X. It wasn't built for Y. It wasn't built for Z.") | When explaining what is missing in a legacy tool. | Dropping the parallelism. Each sentence must start the same way. |
| The metaphor pair | One blunt tool vs. one precise tool ("sledgehammer vs. scalpel," "brakes vs. open road"). | When contrasting what national brands do vs. what local businesses actually need. | Pairing metaphors that do not share a physics. The pair must compare like with unlike. |
| The "I could have been doing this" moment | A founder reflection on what was possible with the right data. | After telling a customer story that lands. | Using it more than once. It only works once per piece. |
| The augmentation restatement | "Not to replace what they have. To make what they have finally work the way it was supposed to. Augmentation, not replacement." | When explaining the platform relationship to agencies or existing tools. | Using it as the opening line. It earns its weight late. |

**Banned as a violation:** one-word fragment paragraphs ("Fine." "Right." "Remarkably." "Clarity.") and any single-word + period sentence. The user called these out as a violation of professional copy on 2026-09-28. Cut them entirely. Use full sentences. If a single-word beat is doing real work, fold it into the surrounding sentence.

## Tics and patterns to avoid

| Pattern | Why it weakens the writing | Preferred response |
|---|---|---|
| Em-dash (the long dash character) | Banned by user rule 2026-09-28. Reads as a model default. | Period, comma, colon, or new sentence. |
| Marketing-website cadence | If a sentence could appear on any GTM platform's About page, it is not this voice. | Rewrite in the founder's stacked-declarative rhythm. |
| Polished-marketing verbs | "Empower," "unlock," "transform," "elevate." These are model defaults. | Concrete verbs: "give," "build," "fix," "put back," "show." |
| Smoothing spoken hedges | In direct speech, "um, you know, like" is the founder, not noise. | Keep in quoted speech. Strip only from editorial copy. |
| Generic Maryland-local color | "Charm City," "Old Line State," "Maryland is a special place." Flat and unmemorable. | Specific, factual Maryland references when they earn it. Skip when they do not. |

## Form vs. register

The founder voice lives in the verbatim signature phrases and the selected signature moves. It does not live in the transcript-style cadence.

Transcript-style cadence (short stacked declaratives, fragment paragraphs, run-on feature lists, spoken rhythm) is the founder's natural mode when talking into a microphone. It is the right register for a recorded interview.

It is the wrong register for the website.

Professional copy is what readers come for. Founder voice is what keeps them reading. Voice colors the copy without obscuring it.

### Rules

- Use the three-beat negative identity once per piece, at the identity boundary. Not in every section.
- Use fragment paragraphs as kickers or closing beats. Not as the structural rhythm.
- Use the run-on feature list at most once per piece, when explaining what is missing in a legacy tool.
- Use the one-word fragment ("Fine." "Right.") at most once per piece.
- Convert the spoken thinking-out-loud cadence into declarative professional copy. Keep the founder's signature phrases. Cut the verbal padding.
- Each section earns its place. If a paragraph can be cut without losing the piece, cut it.

### Why this matters

Captured voice plus transcript cadence reads as someone transcribing a conversation. Captured voice plus professional copy register reads like the founder wrote it on a good day. Same signature phrases. Same mental model. Different surface.

## Positive examples

### Three-beat negative identity

> We're not a marketing agency. We're not a SaaS platform. We're not a bespoke software agency. We're the foundation layer.

**Why it works:** Boundary-drawing at its tightest. Each negative is short, factual, and unembellished. The fourth line reframes what the product actually is. The reader now knows what they are not signing up for.

### Run-on feature list

> Because WordPress wasn't built for what a Maryland business actually needs to do. It wasn't built for instant page load speed. It wasn't built for live editing. It wasn't built for decoupled content management. It wasn't built to be a 24/7 sales website.

**Why it works:** Five sentences, each parallel, each adding one specific limitation. Builds the case without ever saying "limited." Shows it. Reader finishes the list already convinced.

### The "I could have been doing this" moment

> "I could have been doing this." That's the kind of clarity that hits a business owner. Not panic, not regret. Just clarity.

**Why it works:** Honors a real customer moment without sentimentalizing it. Uses a verbatim phrase from the source transcript and lets it carry the weight.

## Negative examples

### Polished-marketing About-page version

> Maryland Insights is a GTM infrastructure platform built by data science and go-to-market engineers. Founded in 2015, we serve Maryland businesses and agencies by providing the foundational tools, hosting, maintenance, and integrations needed to scale. Unlike traditional marketing agencies or SaaS tools, we focus on augmentation over replacement, empowering our partners to make intelligent, data-driven decisions at the state level.

**What is wrong:** No signature phrases. Generic cadence. "Empowering our partners" is a model default. Could appear on any platform's About page. Founder voice is gone.

**Preferred move:** Rewrite in stacked declaratives with the founder's three-beat negative identity, the verbatim "augmentation, not replacement" phrase, and the engineering-tools framing.

## Voice review questions

- Would the founder say this sentence out loud in an interview?
- Does it stack short sentences or run them together in the founder's rhythm?
- Is it carrying a signature phrase, or is it generic?
- Has an em-dash slipped in?
- Does the polish match the founder's spoken cadence, or has it been smoothed into marketing copy?
- Are spoken hedges preserved in direct quotes, stripped in editorial copy?
